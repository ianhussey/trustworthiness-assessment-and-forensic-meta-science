13  Bounds methods ✎ Draft

The methods in this chapter rest on the properties of bounded data described in Chapter 7: on a scale with a minimum and a maximum, the mean and the SD are not free to take any values independently of one another.

13.1 Mean inside range

The simplest bounds check is whether a mean lies within the range of values that its scores could take. The mean of a single item rated from 1 to 5 must lie between 1 and 5. The range of a sum score is the number of items multiplied by the lowest and highest response options, so six items rated from 1 to 5 give sum scores from 6 to 30, and their mean must lie between 6 and 30. A percentage must lie between 0 and 100. When an article reports the smallest and largest observed scores, the mean must also lie between those. And a mean exactly at the minimum or maximum is only possible if every participant had that score, in which case the SD must be zero (Section 7.2).

Working out the range requires knowing the number of items, the response options, how they were coded (for example, from 0 or from 1), and whether items were summed or averaged. Articles sometimes describe these inconsistently, so a mean outside the range shows that either the mean or the description of the measure is wrong, without saying which.

The same reasoning applies to the means of subgroups. When the subgroups together make up the whole sample, without overlap, the mean of the whole sample is a weighted average of the subgroup means, so it must lie between the smallest and the largest of them. The same is true of percentages.

Haller et al. (2022) reported the characteristics of 9,496 participants in their Table 1, both for the whole sample and for eight regions, whose sample sizes add up to 9,496. In the whole sample, 5.1% of participants were divorced, but the table reports 0.0% divorced in every one of the eight regions. Likewise, 5.5% of the whole sample lived with friends or roommates, but 0.0% in every region. A percentage for the whole sample cannot be larger than the percentages in all of the groups that make it up, so these rows of the table cannot be correct. The regression in the same article’s Table 5 includes being divorced and living with friends or roommates as predictors, with estimated coefficients, which suggests that participants in these groups did exist and that the regional zeros are the error.

The same article also describes the ranges of two of its measures inconsistently. The sum score of six prosocial behaviour items, each rated from 1 to 5, is described as ranging from 6 to 36 in the Method section, but from 6 to 30 in Table 4, which is correct. The fourteen well-being items are described as rated on a “6-point” scale from 0 to 6, which has seven points, while the range of the sum score is given as 0 to 70, which is only possible if each item was scored from 0 to 5. None of these means falls outside its range, but the inconsistencies show why the range of a measure should be worked out from its items, not taken from the article.

13.2 SD bounds

On a bounded scale, the mean and the SD are not free to vary independently of one another (Section 7.2). The scale’s minimum and maximum, the sample size, and the mean together set an upper bound on the SD, and once the data are known to be whole numbers, or to include a particular minimum and maximum, they set a lower bound too. A reported SD outside these bounds cannot be correct. Hussey et al. (2026) call this check BRIMMER, for Bounds-Related Inconsistency of Means and Errors Reported, by analogy with GRIMMER (Section 12.3). Its counterpart for the mean alone, BRIM, asks whether the reported mean can be reached at all, as in Section 13.1.

The bounds come from a series of nested constraints, each of which can only tighten them (Hussey et al., 2026):

  • The scale limits. The most spread out any set of scores can be is half at the minimum and half at the maximum. On a scale with range R, the sample SD can never exceed R / √2, which is reached by a sample of two people, one at each end.
  • The sample size. With more people, the largest possible SD falls towards R / 2. For an even sample size it is (R / 2) × √(n / (n − 1)), and for an odd one slightly less, because the people cannot be split evenly between the two ends (Popoviciu, 1935). On a 1–7 scale with 30 people, no SD can exceed 3.05, whatever the mean.
  • The mean. The further the mean is from the middle of the scale, the smaller the largest possible SD, which is √(n / (n − 1) × (max − M)(M − min)). This is the Bhatia–Davis bound of Section 7.2, which Muilwijk (1966) had already derived (Bhatia & Davis, 2000). It can be sharpened slightly, because the number of people at each end of the scale must be a whole number. On a 1–7 scale with 30 people and a mean of 2.97, the SD can be at most 2.84.
  • Integer data. Up to this point, the smallest possible SD is zero: everyone could have the same score. But when every response is a whole number, a mean with a fractional part, such as 2.97, cannot be produced by identical scores, so the SD must be above zero. This floor is usually small: 0.14 in the example.
  • Internal consistency. For a score made up of several items, a reported Cronbach’s α limits how much the items can disagree with one another, which lowers the ceiling and, for whole-number items, raises the floor (Hussey et al., 2026).

The scale limits are not the only minimum and maximum that constrain the SD. Many articles report the smallest and largest scores actually observed, in a table of descriptive statistics or in the text, and these say more than the scale limits do. The scale limits only say that no score lies outside them, whereas an observed minimum and maximum say that at least one person had each of those scores. A sample that includes both a low and a high score cannot have a very small SD, so the observed minimum and maximum raise the floor substantially, especially together with the mean and integer data. They also narrow the range of means that are possible at all, and they lower the ceiling, because the observed range is usually narrower than the scale’s. In the example above, if the article also reported that scores ranged from 1 to 6, the SD could be no smaller than 0.71 and no larger than 2.46.

When checking reported values, the bounds have to take rounding into account: a mean reported as 2.97 could have been anything from 2.965 to 2.975, and the bounds are calculated over that whole interval. Like GRIM and GRIMMER, BRIMMER is a necessary condition rather than a sufficient one. A reported SD outside the bounds is impossible, but one inside them may still not correspond to any real set of whole-number scores. How the bounds combine with the granularity checks, and a further, exact test, BRIMMEST, which settles whether any such set of scores exists, are the subject of Chapter 14.

function sxSet(view, x) {
  // move a range slider to the nearest value it can take, and tell everything that depends on it
  const input = view.querySelector("input[type=range]")
  const min = +input.min, max = +input.max, step = +input.step
  view.value = +Math.min(max, Math.max(min, min + Math.round((x - min) / step) * step)).toFixed(10)
  view.dispatchEvent(new Event("input", {bubbles: true}))
}

function sxFrac(x) {
  // the fractional part, treating values within rounding error of a whole number as whole
  const f = x - Math.floor(x + 1e-9)
  return f < 1e-9 ? 0 : f
}

function sxCeiling(m, n, l, u) {
  // the largest sample SD with this mean, for n values between l and u: as many values as possible at the two ends,
  // and at most one in between (structure S). this sharpens the Bhatia-Davis bound, which assumes the counts at the
  // ends need not be whole numbers
  const R = u - l, S = n * (m - l)
  const nu = Math.min(Math.floor(S / R + 1e-9), n - 1)
  const xr = l + (S - nu * R), nl = n - nu - 1
  return Math.sqrt(Math.max(0, (nl * (l - m) ** 2 + (xr - m) ** 2 + nu * (u - m) ** 2) / (n - 1)))
}

function sxQuasiFloor(m, n) {
  // the smallest sample SD of n values with this mean when all but one are whole numbers (quasi-integer), which gives
  // a floor at every mean that never rules out anything whole-number data could produce
  const d = sxFrac(m), g = sxFrac(n * m)
  return Math.sqrt(Math.max(0, (n * d * (1 - d) - g * (1 - g)) / (n - 1)))
}

function sxVmaxAlpha(m, k, n, itemL, itemU) {
  // the largest possible sum of the item variances of k whole-number items at sum-score mean m (strait's v_max_alpha)
  const h = (itemU - itemL) / n, T = (m - k * itemL) / h, x = T / k
  const j = Math.min(Math.floor(x + 1e-12), n - 1), th = x - j
  let phi = T - Math.floor(T + 1e-12)
  if (phi > 1 - 1e-9) phi = 0
  const A = jj => h * h * jj * (n - jj)
  return Math.max(0, k * ((1 - th) * A(j) + th * A(j + 1)) - h * h * (n - 1) * phi * (1 - phi))
}

function sxMeanBand(n, a, b) {
  // with one value at the observed minimum and one at the observed maximum, the mean cannot be closer to either end
  // than this
  return [a + (b - a) / n, b - (b - a) / n]
}

function sxBoundsAt(mean, design) {
  // the smallest and largest possible sample SD at this mean, in the units of the score
  const {n, k, quasi, toSum, itemLimits, alpha, ab} = design
  const m = mean * toSum, L = itemLimits[0] * k, U = itemLimits[1] * k
  let floor, ceiling, feasible = true
  if (alpha === null) {
    floor = quasi ? sxQuasiFloor(m, n) : 0
    ceiling = sxCeiling(m, n, L, U)
  } else {
    // a reported alpha caps how much the items can disagree: it lowers the ceiling and amplifies the whole-number
    // floor (strait's sd_bounds_alpha, without its enumeration for strictly integer data)
    const D = 1 - (k - 1) / k * alpha
    ceiling = Math.min(sxCeiling(m, n, L, U), Math.sqrt(n / (n - 1) * Math.max(0, (U - m) * (m - L)) / (k * D)))
    floor = 0
    if (quasi) {
      ceiling = Math.min(ceiling, Math.sqrt(n / (n - 1) * sxVmaxAlpha(m, k, n, itemLimits[0], itemLimits[1]) / D))
      floor = sxQuasiFloor(m, n) / Math.sqrt(D)
    }
  }
  if (ab) {
    // one value at each observed extreme, and the other n - 2 as close together as their mean allows
    const [a, b] = ab.map(x => x * toSum), [lo, hi] = sxMeanBand(n, a, b)
    if (m < lo - 1e-9 || m > hi + 1e-9) return {floor: NaN, ceiling: NaN, feasible: false}
    let abFloor
    if (n === 2) abFloor = (b - a) / Math.SQRT2
    else {
      const p = m - a, q = b - m, kk = n - 2
      let ss = p * p + q * q + (p - q) ** 2 / kk
      if (quasi) {
        const inner = (n * m - a - b) / kk, d = sxFrac(inner), g = sxFrac(n * m)
        ss += Math.max(0, kk * d * (1 - d) - g * (1 - g))
      }
      abFloor = Math.sqrt(ss / (n - 1))
    }
    // with alpha as well, the higher floor and the lower ceiling
    floor = Math.max(alpha === null ? 0 : floor, abFloor)
    ceiling = Math.min(alpha === null ? Infinity : ceiling, sxCeiling(m, n, a, b))
  }
  if (floor > ceiling + 1e-9) feasible = false
  return {floor: floor / toSum, ceiling: ceiling / toSum, feasible}
}

function sxParityMax(l, u, n) {
  // the largest SD any sample of this size can have on this scale, whatever its mean (Popoviciu, with the odd-n case)
  return (u - l) / 2 * Math.sqrt(n / (n - 1) * (2 * n * n - 1 + (-1) ** n) / (2 * n * n))
}

function sxVerdict(m, s, here, design, uniform) {
  const f = x => x.toFixed(2), {n, ab, alpha, quasi} = design
  const given = [ab ? "the observed minimum and maximum" : null, alpha !== null ? `an α of ${f(alpha)}` : null].filter(Boolean).join(" and ")
  if (!here.feasible) {
    if (ab && Number.isNaN(here.floor)) {
      const [lo, hi] = sxMeanBand(n, ...ab)
      return {kind: "bad", text: `<strong>Not possible.</strong> With one value at the observed minimum of ${ab[0]} and one at the observed maximum of ${ab[1]}, the mean of ${n} values must lie between ${f(lo)} and ${f(hi)}, whatever the <em>SD</em>.`}
    }
    return {kind: "bad", text: `<strong>Not possible.</strong> No sample of ${n} values with a mean of ${f(m)} satisfies all of the constraints at once: the smallest <em>SD</em> they allow is larger than the largest.`}
  }
  if (s > here.ceiling + 1e-9) return {kind: "bad", text: `<strong>Not possible.</strong> With a mean of ${f(m)}${given ? ` and ${given}` : ""}, the <em>SD</em> of ${n} values can be at most ${f(here.ceiling)}.`}
  if (s < here.floor - 1e-9) return {kind: "bad", text: `<strong>Not possible.</strong> With a mean of ${f(m)}${given ? ` and ${given}` : ""}, the <em>SD</em> of ${n} ${quasi ? "whole-number " : ""}values must be at least ${f(here.floor)}.`}
  if (s > uniform) return {kind: "warn", text: `<strong>Possible, but unusual.</strong> With a mean of ${f(m)}, the <em>SD</em> of ${n} values can be anywhere from ${f(here.floor)} to ${f(here.ceiling)}, but an <em>SD</em> above ${f(uniform)} is more spread out than scores spread evenly across the whole scale. It requires the scores to pile up at the ends of the scale, which is plausible for some measures but not most.`}
  return {kind: "ok", text: `<strong>Possible.</strong> With a mean of ${f(m)}${given ? ` and ${given}` : ""}, the <em>SD</em> of ${n} ${quasi ? "whole-number " : ""}values can be anywhere from ${f(here.floor)} to ${f(here.ceiling)}.`}
}

Start with the mean at 3 on the 1–7 scale and raise the SD until the point turns red: the largest possible SD is close to the Bhatia–Davis bound, but slightly below it, because the number of people at each end of the scale has to be a whole number. On the way, the point turns amber as it crosses the uniform-spread bound, where the scores would have to pile up at the ends of the scale. Move the mean towards either end and the largest possible SD falls. With whole-number responses, the smallest possible SD is zero only when the mean is a whole number, and rises to a small peak between whole numbers. Now choose a mean of three items and tick Cronbach’s α: the higher the α, the lower the ceiling, because items that agree with one another cannot produce scores as spread out as items that disagree. Finally, tick Observed minimum and maximum: with scores known to include both the minimum and the maximum given, the band lifts well off the floor, the ceiling falls, and means near the ends of the scale become impossible.

13.3 Estimates inside confidence intervals

Most estimates in quantitative research are reported with a confidence interval: a mean difference, a regression coefficient, an odds ratio, or a correlation, followed by its lower and upper bounds. The estimate and its interval are calculated from the same data by the same analysis, so they are not free to take any values relative to one another. This gives two checks that need nothing but the three reported numbers. The first is a possibility check: is the estimate inside its interval? The second is a plausibility check: is the estimate in the middle of its interval, as it should be for many common methods, once rounding is taken into account?

13.3.1 Possibility: is the estimate inside its interval?

For almost every method used in practice, a confidence interval contains its own point estimate. An estimate outside its interval is therefore close to proof that at least one of the three numbers is wrong. The exceptions are rare and exotic, such as percentile bootstrap intervals for heavily biased statistics, and an article that used such a method would be expected to say so. More common explanations are a typo, digits transposed while copying, values copied from the wrong row or the wrong model, or bounds and estimates entered in the wrong order. An estimate that equals one of its bounds is technically inside the interval, but for most methods it is no more plausible than one outside it.

Georgescu & Wren (2018) automatically extracted 244,750 ratios (odds ratios, risk ratios, and hazard ratios) and their 95% confidence intervals from the abstracts of articles indexed in MEDLINE. Between 0.4% and 0.6% of the ratios, depending on the type, were outside their own intervals. A reanalysis of their data for this book, using the 151,461 ratios whose values could be checked with the rounding of each value taken into account, found that 0.43% were outside their intervals, and that 0.8% of the abstracts contained at least one such ratio. These are small percentages, but they come from abstracts, which usually contain an article’s main results, and almost every one of them points to an error, in the article or, occasionally, in the automated extraction.

13.3.2 Plausibility: is the estimate in the middle of its interval?

Many confidence intervals are symmetric around their estimate. The usual interval for a mean, a mean difference, or a coefficient in a linear regression is the estimate plus or minus a multiple of its standard error, so the estimate lies exactly halfway between the bounds. Intervals for ratios such as odds ratios, risk ratios, and hazard ratios are usually calculated in the same way on the log scale and then exponentiated, so the estimate lies halfway between the bounds on the log scale: it is the geometric mean of the two bounds, √(lower × upper), rather than their arithmetic mean. This was the basis of the recalculations by Georgescu & Wren (2018).

Rounding means that the reported estimate need not be exactly in the middle of the reported bounds. Each of the three numbers was rounded separately, so the check asks whether any combination of unrounded values that would round to the three reported numbers places the estimate exactly in the middle. For example, an odds ratio reported as 8.4 [3.7, 19.2] passes: the geometric mean of the bounds is 8.43, which rounds to 8.4. An odds ratio of 1.52 [1.18, 1.93] does not: whatever the unrounded bounds were, their geometric mean lies between 1.504 and 1.514, and no unrounded estimate between 1.515 and 1.525 can equal it. The difference is small, but it is larger than rounding can produce.

Unlike an estimate outside its interval, an estimate that is off-centre is not proof of an error, because many legitimate methods produce intervals that are not symmetric around their estimate on any simple scale. Examples include profile likelihood intervals, which R uses by default for the coefficients of logistic and other generalised linear models; exact intervals, such as Clopper–Pearson intervals for proportions and conditional intervals for odds ratios from small samples; Wilson intervals for proportions; intervals for correlations calculated through Fisher’s z transformation, which are symmetric on the z scale but not on the scale of the correlation; intervals for Standardized Mean Differences based on the noncentral t distribution; bootstrap intervals, which can be asymmetric even when the model itself assumes normally distributed errors, because they follow the shape of the bootstrap distribution rather than a formula; and Bayesian credible intervals from skewed posterior distributions. Whether an off-centre estimate is a concern therefore depends on the metric and on the method used to calculate its interval. An off-centre coefficient from an ordinary least squares regression with standard intervals is a strong sign of an error. An off-centre odds ratio from a profile likelihood interval is expected.

In the reanalysis of the data of Georgescu & Wren (2018), 92.7% of the ratios were centred on the log scale given rounding, 6.8% were inside their intervals but off-centre, and 0.43% were outside their intervals. 13.4 Coefficient bounds

Many coefficients have bounds that follow from how they are defined, whatever was measured. A correlation must lie between −1 and 1, and a proportion or an R2 between 0 and 1. Reliability, defined as the proportion of the variance in scores that is true-score variance, also lies between 0 and 1, but the coefficients used to estimate it are bounded less tightly. Cronbach’s α cannot exceed 1, but it has no lower bound: it is negative whenever the items are, on average, negatively correlated with one another, as happens when reverse-keyed items have not been reverse-scored, and two items that correlate −.90 give an α of −18. A negative α is therefore possible, but it shows that the items do not measure one thing in the way they were scored, and so it is a reason to look closely. A reported value outside a coefficient’s bounds cannot be correct. Table 13.1 lists the bounds of some statistics that are common in psychology and medicine.

Some of the statistics in Table 13.1 are bias-corrected or variance-based estimates that can legitimately fall slightly below zero when the quantity they estimate is close to zero, such as adjusted R2, ω2, and the intraclass correlation. Being able to fall slightly below zero does not mean that they have no bounds. Most of them have a lower bound that follows from the sample size or design, and it is usually close to zero. An R2 of −4.1 is impossible, because R2 cannot be negative at all, and an adjusted R2 that negative would need an R2 of zero and almost as many predictors as people. A single-measure intraclass correlation of −9.1 is also impossible in practice: the one-way and consistency forms cannot fall below −1 with any number of raters, and the agreement form can only do so in samples of a few subjects. Only a few statistics, such as Cronbach’s α and the average-measure intraclass correlation, have no lower bound at all, and even for these, a value far below zero is implausible without a specific explanation.

Values this obviously wrong still reach print. Hussein et al. (2022) compared machine learning models for classifying lung diseases from chest X-rays, and reported each model’s accuracy, precision, recall, and F1 score. The F1 score is the harmonic mean of precision and recall, 2 × precision × recall / (precision + recall), so it must lie between them, and like them between 0 and 1. As a comment on PubPeer pointed out, every F1 score in the article’s Tables 2 to 4 is above 1, ranging from 1.40 to 3.64. They are not percentages, because the precision and recall in the same rows are reported as proportions. Almost all of them are four times the recall in the same row: one model was reported with a precision of .6900, a recall of .6891, and an F1 score of 2.7563, whereas the F1 score implied by its precision and recall is .6895. Whatever the cause, none of the F1 scores can be correct. Later comments on the same article raise further questions about the other metrics in these tables.

Values that only break a bound once they are combined with other reported numbers are probably more common, because no one checks the combination. The next section describes one such check.

13.4.1 Disattenuated correlations

Measurement error weakens correlations. If two measures are not perfectly reliable, the correlation between them is smaller in size than the correlation between the constructs they measure, a bias known as attenuation. Spearman (1904) showed how to correct for it: divide the observed correlation by the square root of the product of the two measures’ reliabilities,

\[ r_{xy}^{\text{true}} = \frac{r_{xy}}{\sqrt{r_{xx} \, r_{yy}}}, \]

where rxy is the observed correlation and rxx and ryy are the reliabilities of the two measures, such as Cronbach’s α or a test–retest correlation. The disattenuated correlation is an estimate of the correlation between the constructs, and like any correlation it must lie between −1 and 1.

This gives a check that uses three numbers which articles often report together. Rearranging the formula, a correlation can only be as large as the square root of the product of the reliabilities: two measures with reliabilities of .70 can correlate at most .70, however closely related the constructs are. A reported correlation of .80 between them implies a disattenuated correlation of 1.14, which is impossible. Either the correlation, at least one of the reliabilities, or both are wrong, or the reliabilities were estimated in a different sample from the correlation.

Whether a disattenuated correlation outside −1 to 1 shows that the reported numbers are impossible, or only that they are implausible, depends on where the reliabilities come from. When the correlation and both reliabilities were estimated in the same sample, as when an article reports all three, they describe the same data, and the check is a possibility check: the numbers cannot all be correct, subject to the caveats about sampling error and the type of reliability below. When the reliabilities come from other samples, such as a test manual, earlier studies, or a meta-analysis, the check is a plausibility check. The reliabilities then describe what is typical for the measures, and the measures may have been more or less reliable in the sample at hand, so a disattenuated correlation outside −1 to 1 shows that the reported correlation is implausible given what is known about the measures, not that it is impossible. For the same reason, √(rxx ryy) is a ceiling on the correlation to be expected between two measures, not on the correlation that can be observed in any one sample, which varies around its expected value.

The plausibility check can be applied to meta-analyses as well as to single studies. Vahey et al. (2015) reported a meta-analytic correlation of r = .45 between the Implicit Relational Assessment Procedure (IRAP), a reaction-time-based measure, and clinical criterion measures. A later meta-analysis estimated the IRAP’s internal consistency at α = .27 and its test–retest reliability at .18 (Hussey & Drake, 2020). As Hussey (2025) pointed out, these estimates are incompatible with one another. Even if the criterion measures were assumed to be highly reliable (ryy = .90), the disattenuated correlation would be .91 using the IRAP’s internal consistency and 1.12 using its test–retest reliability. With a lower and arguably more plausible criterion reliability of .70, it would be 1.04 or 1.27. Either the meta-analytic correlation was too large, or the meta-analytic reliabilities were too small. A reanalysis of the original meta-analysis found serious errors that generally inflated the effect size, and a new meta-analysis calculated to show their combined impact produced an estimate half the size, r = .22 (Hussey, 2025).

Some points to keep in mind when using this check:

  • Reliabilities from other samples are still informative. When an article does not report reliabilities, estimates from a test manual, earlier studies, or a reliability generalization meta-analysis can stand in for them, and the check becomes a plausibility check. It is often said that reliability is a property of scores in a particular sample rather than of a measure, but this is too strong. Reliability generalization meta-analyses, which pool the reliabilities reported for a measure across many samples, find that reliability varies between samples but that the measure itself also contributes to it (Vacha-Haase, 1998): some measures are reliable in most of the samples in which they are used, and others rarely are. A correlation that would be impossible given a measure’s typical reliability is not proof of an error, but it is a useful intuition pump and a fair reason to ask questions.
  • Small excesses can arise from sampling error. The correlation and the reliabilities are all estimates, and in small samples a disattenuated correlation slightly above 1 can occur by chance even when every reported number is correct. A disattenuated correlation of 1.02 in a sample of 50 is weak evidence of a problem. One of 1.30 in a sample of 500 is strong evidence.
  • Rounding is not taken into account. The app uses the values exactly as entered. A disattenuated correlation just above 1, calculated from values rounded to two decimal places, may be within rounding of a possible one, so the app is better treated as an intuition pump than as a formal test.
  • The type of reliability matters. Internal consistency coefficients such as Cronbach’s α can underestimate or overestimate reliability, depending on whether their assumptions hold, which changes the disattenuated correlation in turn. When an article reports more than one kind of reliability, check the result with each.
  • A disattenuated correlation close to 1 is itself informative. Even when it is possible, a disattenuated correlation near 1 suggests that the two measures assess the same construct, which may sit uneasily with an article’s claim that they measure distinct things.

  1. An article reports an F1 score of 2.76, alongside a precision of .69 and a recall of .69. The F1 score could be correct.
  2. An adjusted R2 slightly below zero can be correct.
  3. An ordinary R2 can be negative when a model fits very badly.
  4. Cronbach’s α can be negative.
  5. Two measures each have a reliability of .70. What is the largest correlation to expect between them?
  6. A correlation of .80 is reported between two measures whose reliabilities, taken from their test manuals, are both .70. This shows that the correlation is impossible.
  1. True. The range of a sum score is the number of items times the lowest and highest response options: 6 × 1 = 6 and 6 × 5 = 30. Work the range out from the items rather than taking it from the article, which may describe it inconsistently (Section 13.1).
  2. True. The mean of the whole sample is a weighted average of the subgroup means, so it cannot be smaller than all of them or larger than all of them. The same is true of percentages (Section 13.1).
  3. False. However small or large the regions are, a percentage for the whole sample cannot be larger than the percentages in every one of the groups that make it up. If every region has 0.0%, so must the whole sample (Section 13.1).
  4. Impossible, whatever the mean. With 30 people on a 1–7 scale, no SD can exceed 3.05, which is reached with half the people at each end of the scale (Section 13.2).
  5. False. An SD of 0 means that everyone had the same score, and identical whole numbers cannot have a mean with a fractional part, such as 2.97. Whole-number data raise the floor above zero at every mean that is not a whole number (Section 13.2).
  6. True. A sample that includes both the observed minimum and the observed maximum cannot have a very small SD. On a 1–7 scale with 30 people and a mean of 2.97, reporting a minimum of 1 and a maximum of 6 raises the floor from 0.14 to 0.71 (Section 13.2).
  7. True. A reported α limits how much the items can disagree with one another, which lowers the ceiling on the SD of the total or mean score (Section 13.2).
  8. False. BRIMMER is a necessary condition, not a sufficient one. A reported SD inside the bounds may still not correspond to any real set of whole-number scores; only BRIMMEST settles that (Section 13.2, Chapter 14).
  9. Almost always an error. For almost every method used in practice, a confidence interval contains its own estimate. The exceptions are rare, and an article that used one would be expected to say so (Section 13.3.1).
  10. False. Intervals for ratios are usually calculated on the log scale, so the estimate lies halfway between the bounds on that scale: it is the geometric mean of the bounds, not their arithmetic mean (Section 13.3.2).
  11. False. Many legitimate methods produce asymmetric intervals, such as profile likelihood, exact, Wilson, and bootstrap intervals. Whether an off-centre estimate is a concern depends on the metric and on the method the article used (Section 13.3.2).
  12. The number of decimal places sets how much rounding is allowed for. A bound reported as 0.30 could have been anything from 0.295 to 0.305, but one entered as 0.3 could have been anything from 0.25 to 0.35 (Section 13.3).
  13. False. Even if the columns were simply swapped, readers and meta-analysts usually take reported values at face value, and a meta-analyst who uses the wrong estimate or calculates the wrong standard error will distort the pooled results (Section 13.3).
  14. False. The F1 score is the harmonic mean of precision and recall, so it must lie between them, and like them between 0 and 1. With a precision and recall of .69, the F1 score is .69 (Section 13.4).
  15. True. Adjusted R2 is bias-corrected, and can fall slightly below zero when R2 is close to zero. Its lower bound depends on the number of predictors and the sample size, and is usually close to zero (Section 13.4).
  16. False. R2 is a proportion of variance, so it lies between 0 and 1. A reported R2 below zero cannot be correct (Section 13.4).
  17. True. Cronbach’s α has no lower bound, and is negative when the items are on average negatively correlated, usually because reverse-keyed items were not reverse-scored. A negative α is possible, but a sign of a problem with the scale or its scoring (Section 13.4).
  18. .70. The largest correlation to expect is the square root of the product of the reliabilities: √(.70 × .70) = .70, however closely related the constructs are (Section 13.4.1).
  19. False. The disattenuated correlation would be 1.14, but because the reliabilities come from other samples, this is a plausibility check, not a possibility check: the measures may have been more reliable in this sample. It is a good reason to ask questions, but not proof of an error (Section 13.4.1).
