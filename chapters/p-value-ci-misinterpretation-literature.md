# Misinterpretations of p values and confidence intervals: literature notes

Compiled 29–30 September 2026. Items marked † were drawn from general knowledge rather than verified by search; check before citing.

---

## Part A. Empirical literature on misinterpretation

### 1. Surveys of researchers, teachers and students on p values (the Oakes paradigm)

- **Oakes (1986)** gave 70 academic psychologists a significant *t*-test result and six interpretive statements, all false. Most endorsed at least one. As Cohen (1994) summarises it, 68 of 70 read p as the probability that H0 is true, and 42 believed p = .01 implied a 99% chance that a replication would be significant.
- **Falk & Greenbaum (1995)** replicated the result in Israel.
- **Haller & Krauss (2002)** replicated it in Germany. Even professors and lecturers teaching statistics endorsed false statements. In Gigerenzer's (2004) report of these data (t = 2.7, df = 18, p = .01), none of 44 students rejected all six statements, compared with 4 of 39 non-statistics faculty and 6 of 30 statistics-teaching faculty.
- **Badenes-Ribera et al. (2015)** replicated the result in Spain.
- **Badenes-Ribera et al. (2016)** surveyed 164 academic psychologists in Italy and Chile. The inverse-probability fallacy was the most common error, followed by the replication fallacy.
- **Lyu, Peng & Hu (2018)** surveyed 362 psychology researchers and students in China. 99% misinterpreted at least one p-value statement.
- **Lyu, Xu, Zhao, Zuo & Hu (2020)** surveyed 1,479 researchers and students across fields in China.
  - Misinterpretation did not vary by degree, field or career stage.
  - More than half treated 1−p or 1−α as the probability of successful replication.
  - They also measured respondents' confidence and found people were largely unaware of their own errors.
- **Gigerenzer (2018)**, "Statistical rituals: The replication delusion" (*AMPPS*), compiles the country-level results into one table. It is the best single overview.
- A meta-analysis of the Oakes, Falk & Greenbaum and Haller & Krauss data (Perezgonzalez, WikiofScience) put the overall misunderstanding rate at about 91%.

### 2. Confidence intervals

- **Belia, Fidler, Williams & Cumming (2005)** asked authors in psychology, behavioural neuroscience and medicine to adjust two means with error bars until they looked just significantly different at p < .05. The researchers placed CIs and SE bars poorly and often used a rigid "no overlap = significant" rule.
- **Cumming, Williams & Fidler (2004)** documented a replication misconception. The average probability that one 95% CI captures the next sample mean is about 83%, not 95%.
- **Fidler (2005)** found that more than half of students read a CI as a range of observed scores, as scores within one SD, or as plausible values of the sample mean.
- **Fidler & Loftus (2009)** found that about 30% of students read a CI that includes the H0 value as "no effect".
- **Hoekstra, Morey, Rouder & Wagenmakers (2014)** built a CI analogue of the Oakes questionnaire with six false statements. Researchers barely outperformed first-year students, and experience did not predict accuracy.
- **Morey, Hoekstra, Rouder, Lee & Wagenmakers (2016)**, "The fallacy of placing confidence in confidence intervals", is the theoretical companion. It argues that the "precision" and "plausible values" readings are not licensed by CI theory itself.

### 3. Textbooks

- **Cassidy, Dimova, Giguère, Spence & Stanley (2019)** examined 30 introductory psychology textbooks, including US and Canadian best-sellers. 89% defined or explained statistical significance incorrectly, most often through the odds-against-chance fallacy. The authors conclude that students may be accurately recalling incorrect teaching.
- Older audits of statistics textbooks:
  - Brewer (1985)†
  - Gliner, Leech & Morgan (2002)†
  - Huberty (1993)†
- Haller & Krauss (2002) and Gigerenzer (2004) also quote textbook errors.

### 4. Errors in the published literature and judgment tasks

- **Murphy, Merz, Reimann & Fernández (2025)** estimated that 76–85% of psychology articles from 2009 to 2021 that discussed a nonsignificant result misread it as no effect. The rate did not change significantly over time.
- **Hoekstra, Finch, Kiers & Johnson (2006)**, "Probability as certainty": dichotomous thinking in published articles†.
- **Nieuwenhuis, Forstmann & Wagenmakers (2011)**: erroneous comparisons of effects based on whether each was significant†.
- **Gelman & Stern (2006)**: the difference between "significant" and "not significant" is not itself statistically significant.
- **McShane & Gal (2016, *Management Science*; 2017, *JASA*)**: researchers misread plain descriptions of data depending on whether an accompanying p value fell above or below .05.
- The cliff effect: Rosenthal & Gaito (1963); Poitevineau & Lecoutre (2001)†.
- **Lecoutre, Poitevineau & Lecoutre (2003)**: statisticians are not immune either†.

### 5. Medicine

- **Windish, Huot & Green (2007, *JAMA*)**: medicine residents' biostatistics knowledge†.
- **Anaesthesiology pilot survey (PMC5660771)**: only 28% identified the correct CI definition.
- **Survey of 898 clinicians (*Cognitive Research: Principles and Implications*, 2023)**: assessed skill and confidence together on vaccine efficacy, p values and diagnostic tests.
- **Internal medicine physician survey (*J Clin Epidemiol*, 2026)**: clinicians relied on statistical significance rather than the size and direction of effects when CIs were wide.
- **Gagnier & Morgenstern (2017, *JBJS*)**: misconceptions and misuses of p values in orthopaedics.

### 6. Critiques of the measurement paradigm

- **García-Pérez & Alcalá-Quintana (2016, *Front. Psychol.*)** argue that CIs have two legitimate interpretations. On that view, endorsing some of Hoekstra's items does not demonstrate misinterpretation, and true/false items do not capture how scholars naturally phrase interpretations. Their replication added correct items so that misinterpretation could be separated from not knowing.
- **Miller & Ulrich (2016)** also challenged the Hoekstra statements.
- **McShane & Gal (2017)** note that as worded, question 1 of the Oakes/Gigerenzer set appears to ask about unknown population parameters.
- **Implication:** prevalence figures such as "~90–99% endorse at least one false statement" depend on the instrument used. The direction of the finding is robust; the exact percentages are not well-measured prevalence estimates.

### 7. Conceptual catalogues (not empirical)

- Greenland, Senn, Rothman, Carlin, Poole, Goodman & Altman (2016). A guide to 25 misinterpretations.
- Goodman (2008), "A dirty dozen"†.
- Nickerson (2000, *Psych Methods*)†.
- Wasserstein & Lazar (2016), the ASA statement.
- Castro Sotos et al. (2007, *Educational Research Review*), a review of student misconceptions†.

## Part B. Authoritative definitions of the p value (positive exemplars)

### 1. Consensus and practitioner definitions

- **ASA Statement (Wasserstein & Lazar, 2016, p. 131).**
  - The statement labels its definition "informal". It defines the p value as "the probability under a specified statistical model that a statistical summary of the data" would equal or exceed its observed value.
  - It names the whole model, not only H0.
  - It comes with six principles. The first says p values indicate how incompatible data are with a specified model. The second says they do not give the probability that a hypothesis is true, or that chance alone produced the data.
- **Greenland et al. (2016).**
  - Defines p as the probability that the test statistic would be at least as large as observed "if every model assumption were correct, including the test hypothesis."
  - A small p flags the data as unusual under the whole model, but does not say which assumption failed.
  - This is the better exemplar if assumptions other than H0 (selection, analytic flexibility) should stay visible.
- **ASA President's Task Force (Benjamini et al., 2021, *Ann. Appl. Stat.*)**†. A shorter follow-up that is less critical of p values.

### 2. Formal definitions (textbooks)

- **Casella & Berger, *Statistical Inference* (2nd ed., §8.3.4)**†. A p value is a statistic in [0,1]. It is valid if, for every θ in the null space, P(p ≤ α) ≤ α; that is, it is super-uniform under H0. This is the most precise definition, and it shows why "probability H0 is true" is a category error.
- **Lehmann & Romano, *Testing Statistical Hypotheses***†. The Neyman–Pearson version: the smallest significance level at which H0 would be rejected given the observed data.
- **Cox (2006), *Principles of Statistical Inference*; Cox & Hinkley (1974)**†. The Fisherian significance-test version: the probability of a test statistic at least as extreme, under the null distribution.

### 3. Compatibility reframings

- **Greenland (2019), "Valid P-values behave exactly as they should"** (*Am. Stat.* 73, sup1). Documents how much definitions vary even across authoritative sources. Proposes describing p as the degree of compatibility between H and the data given the model, from 0 (data impossible under the model) to 1 (no incompatibility detected).
- **Rafi & Greenland (2020, *BMC Med Res Methodol*)**. Compatibility and S-values (−log₂ p).
- **Greenland (2023, *Scand. J. Stat.* 50:54–88)**. Distinguishes divergence p values (a continuous measure) from decision p values (a procedure with α control). Many "correct" definitions mix the two.

### 4. Note on using these as scoring standards

The ASA and Greenland definitions are model-relative and Fisherian in spirit. Casella–Berger and Lehmann–Romano are procedural. A textbook definition can be correct by one standard and incomplete by the other, so a two-tier coding scheme is more defensible than a single gold-standard sentence:

- **Tier 1 (necessary):** the probability is of the data or a statistic, is conditional on the model and H0, and includes "at least as extreme".
- **Tier 2 (desirable):** the definition names the assumptions beyond H0.
