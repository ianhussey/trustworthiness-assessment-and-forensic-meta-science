4  Interpretation methods ✎ Draft

4.1 (Mis)interpretation of p-values

Before assessing whether an article’s conclusions follow from its results, it is worth checking whether we ourselves can reliably say what its results mean. The p-value is the most widely reported inferential statistic in psychology and health research, and it is also among the most widely misunderstood. This is not a problem confined to students: for four decades, surveys have found that most researchers, and most people who teach statistics, endorse at least one false statement about what a significant p-value tells them.

4.1.1 Test yourself

The questionnaire below was first used by Oakes (1986) with academic psychologists, and has since been reused, with minor changes, in Israel (Falk & Greenbaum, 1995), Germany (Haller & Krauss, 2002), Spain (Badenes-Ribera et al., 2015), and China (Lyu et al., 2018, 2020). The wording here follows Haller and Krauss (2002).

Answer all six statements, then click the button at the bottom to see how you did.

Suppose you have a treatment that you suspect may alter performance on a certain task. You compare the means of your control and experimental groups (say 20 subjects in each sample). Further, suppose you use a simple independent means t-test and your result is (t = 2.7, d.f. = 18, p = 0.01). Please mark each of the statements below as “true” or “false”. “False” means that the statement does not follow logically from the above premises. Also note that one, several or none of the statements may be correct.

  1. You have absolutely disproved the null hypothesis (that is, there is no difference between the population means).
  2. You have found the probability of the null hypothesis being true.
  3. You have absolutely proved your experimental hypothesis (that there is a difference between the population means).
  4. You can deduce the probability of the experimental hypothesis being true.
  5. You know, if you decide to reject the null hypothesis, the probability that you are making the wrong decision.
  6. You have a reliable experimental finding in the sense that if, hypothetically, the experiment were repeated a great number of times, you would obtain a significant result on 99% of occasions.

All six statements are false. Each one corresponds to a recognisable fallacy.

  1. Certainty (disproof). A significance test gives a probabilistic statement about data, not a proof. Data this extreme occur under the null model 1% of the time, so the null hypothesis has not been disproved.
  2. Inverse probability. The p-value is the probability of data at least this extreme given the null model, \(P(D \mid H_0)\). It is not the probability of the null hypothesis given the data, \(P(H_0 \mid D)\). Getting from one to the other requires a prior probability for \(H_0\), which a significance test does not use.
  3. Certainty (proof). The mirror image of statement 1. Rejecting the null does not prove the alternative, and even if a difference exists, it does not establish that the experimental hypothesis’s explanation for it is correct.
  4. Inverse probability, again. The probability of the experimental hypothesis is no more deducible from p than the probability of the null is.
  5. Error-rate confusion. This reads p as the probability that rejecting \(H_0\) is a mistake, i.e., \(P(H_0 \mid \text{significant result})\). That is the false discovery risk, which depends on the prior probability of \(H_0\) and on statistical power, neither of which is given. The p-value is also not the Type I error rate: α is fixed before the study and describes the long-run behaviour of the procedure, not the probability of error in this particular decision.
  6. Replication fallacy. 1 − p is not the probability of a significant replication. The replication probability depends on the true effect size and the replication’s power. If the observed effect were exactly the true effect, a same-sized replication would be significant only about 70–75% of the time; and because significant results tend to overestimate effects, the real figure is usually lower.

4.1.2 Prevalence of (mis)interpretations of p-values

If you marked any statement as true, you are in the majority.

>90%

of psychology students and professors agree with one or more of these misinterpretations of significant p-values, most often by describing p as the probability that the results occurred by chance (see Gigerenzer, 2018 for review).

  • Oakes (1986) found that 97% of the 70 academic psychologists he surveyed endorsed at least one of the statements (Gigerenzer, 2018). As summarised by Cohen (1994), 68 of 70 interpreted p as the probability that \(H_0\) is true, and 42 believed a p of .01 implied a 99% chance that a replication would be significant.
  • Haller and Krauss (2002) gave the questionnaire to students, methodology instructors, and scientists in Germany. As reported by Gigerenzer (2004), none of the 44 psychology students rejected all six statements, compared with 4 of 39 scientists who did not teach statistics and 6 of 30 methodology instructors who did.
  • Lyu et al. (2018) found that 99% of 362 psychology researchers and students in China endorsed at least one false statement. In a larger sample of 1,479 researchers and students across fields, Lyu et al. (2020) found that misinterpretation did not vary meaningfully by degree, field, or career stage, and that respondents were largely unaware of their errors.
  • Badenes-Ribera et al. (2016) found the inverse-probability fallacy to be the most common error among academic psychologists in Italy and Chile, followed by the replication fallacy.

Table 4.1 summarises these studies using Gigerenzer’s (2018) Table 2, showing the percentage of respondents in each sample who endorsed at least one of the six false statements in the quiz above.

4.1.2.1 How much should we trust these prevalence estimates?

The direction of this literature is robust, but the precise percentages should be treated with some caution. The questionnaires consist entirely of false statements, which means they cannot distinguish a respondent who misunderstands p-values from one who is uncertain, reads the items charitably, or assumes that at least one statement must be true. McShane and Gal (2017) also note that, as worded, some items can be read as questions about unknown population parameters rather than about the observed result. Similar criticisms have been made of the equivalent questionnaire for confidence intervals (García-Pérez & Alcalá-Quintana, 2016; Miller & Ulrich, 2016). Figures such as “90–99% of researchers endorse at least one false statement” are therefore better read as evidence that misinterpretation is common across fields, career stages, and countries than as well-measured prevalence estimates.

4.1.3 Errors in intro to psych textbooks’ definitions

The errors are not confined to survey responses. They are also in the textbooks.

89%

of introductory psychology textbooks that defined or explained statistical significance did so incorrectly, most often by describing p as the probability that the results occurred by chance (Cassidy et al., 2019).

4.2 Misinterpretations in the literature

Students who give these answers may therefore be accurately recalling what they were taught. The errors also reach the published literature.

76–85%

of psychology articles that discussed a nonsignificant result interpreted it as evidence that the effect was absent, with no significant change between 2009 and 2021 (Murphy et al., 2025).

4.3 What a p-value is

It’s important to start by emphasizing that I and this book should not be considered the authoritative source of the definition and interpretation of p-values. For these, see the original sources cited below.

4.3.1 The formal definition

The definition found in most statistics textbooks descends from Fisher’s significance tests and is stated formally by Cox (2006). Choose a test statistic T that measures how far the data depart from what the null hypothesis predicts. The p-value is the probability, calculated assuming the null hypothesis is true, of obtaining a value of T at least as extreme as the one actually observed:

\[ p = P(T \geq t_{\text{obs}};\ H_0) \]

The semicolon rather than a conditional bar (|) is deliberate. In the frequentist framework \(H_0\) is not a random event with a probability of its own; it is an assumption under which the probability is calculated. Three ingredients are always involved:

  1. A test statistic, such as t, F, or χ², which summarises the data. For a two-sided test, T is the absolute value, e.g., |t|, so that departures in either direction count as extreme.
  2. A reference distribution for that statistic, derived by assuming \(H_0\) is true.
  3. A tail: the probability of the observed value or anything more extreme, not of the observed value alone.

Applied to the quiz scenario: if there were truly no difference between the population means, and the other assumptions of the t-test held, a t statistic at least as far from zero as 2.7 would arise in about 1.5% of repeated studies with 18 degrees of freedom. (The questionnaire’s p = .01 is rounded; the exact two-sided value for t(18) = 2.7 is .015.)

This definition is the key to every statement in the quiz. The p-value is calculated by assuming \(H_0\) is true, so it cannot also be the probability that \(H_0\) is true. As Greenland et al. (2016) put it, it is hard to see how a probability deduced from a set of assumptions could be the probability of those same assumptions.

4.3.1.1 “The model” is the null model

The American Statistical Association’s statement on p-values (Wasserstein & Lazar, 2016) gives an informal definition that is often quoted:

“the [long-run] probability under a specified statistical model that a statistical summary of the data … would be equal to or more extreme than its observed value.”

American Statistical Association’s statement on p-values (Wasserstein & Lazar, 2016).

Many readers initially struggle with what “a specified statistical model” refers to. It is not the researcher’s hypothesis, the alternative hypothesis, but rather the null model: the null hypothesis together with every other assumption needed to derive the reference distribution. The statement’s first principle makes this explicit, describing the most common context as “a model, constructed under a set of assumptions, together with a so-called ‘null hypothesis’”. For the independent-samples t-test in the quiz, the null model includes:

  • The null hypothesis itself: the population means are equal.
  • Distributional assumptions: the outcome is normally distributed within each group, with equal variances.
  • Design assumptions: observations are independent, and participants were randomly sampled or randomly assigned.
  • Analysis and reporting assumptions: the test was planned before seeing the data, and this p-value was not selected for reporting from among many because it was small.

Greenland et al. (2016) build this into their definition.

The p-value is “the [long-run] probability that the chosen test statistic would have been at least as large as its observed value if every model assumption were correct, including the test hypothesis.”

Greenland et al. (2016)

In their terms, \(H_0\) is just the one assumption that has been targeted for testing (the “test hypothesis”); the p-value itself tests all of them at once.

4.3.2 What we want to know versus what a p-value tells us: the inverse probability fallacy

Most of the misinterpretations in the quiz come from a mismatch between the question researchers want answered and the question a p-value actually answers.

What most researchers want to know:

Given my data,
how probable is it that my hypothesis is true?

This can be formalised as \(P(H_1 \mid D)\), the probability of the alternative hypothesis given the data. Because the null and alternative hypotheses are mutually exclusive and exhaustive (the effect is either zero or it is not), this is the same question as how probable the null hypothesis is, since \(P(H_1 \mid D) = 1 - P(H_0 \mid D)\).

What a p-value actually tells us:

Given that the null model is true,
how improbable are data like mine?

This can be formalised as \(P(D \mid H_0)\), the probability of the data given the null hypothesis. Here \(D\) is shorthand for “data at least as extreme as those observed”, and the conditional bar is used loosely for “assuming”, as it usually is in teaching (see the note on the semicolon above).

Significance testing then adds an inferential step that is not part of the probability calculation at all:

Given that the null model is true, how improbable are data like mine?
If very improbable, we will act as if the alternative hypothesis is true.

This decision rule is what \(\alpha\) controls: in the long run, no more than 5% of tests of true null hypotheses will be significant (given the test’s assumptions and no bias such as uncorrected multiple testing or p-hacking). It does not say how probable any particular hypothesis is.

The p-value, \(P(D \mid H_0)\), and the quantity researchers want, \(P(H_1 \mid D)\), differ in two ways, and misinterpretations usually slip across both at once:

  1. The direction of the conditional is reversed. \(P(D \mid H_0)\) is the probability of the data given the null; \(P(H_0 \mid D)\) is the probability of the null given the data. These can differ enormously. The probability that a person is a smoker given that they have lung cancer is high; the probability that a person has lung cancer given that they smoke is much lower. Treating one as the other is known as the inverse probability fallacy or the transposed conditional.
  2. The complement is then taken. Having read p as \(P(H_0 \mid D)\), it is a short step to reading \(1 - p\) as \(P(H_1 \mid D)\): “p = .01, so there is a 99% chance the effect is real.”

Quiz statement 2 is the first step: reading p as \(P(H_0 \mid D)\). Statement 4 is both steps: reading \(1 - p\) as \(P(H_1 \mid D)\). Statement 5 reads p as \(P(H_0 \mid \text{significant result})\), the probability that a rejection of \(H_0\) is mistaken. All three treat a probability of data as a probability of a hypothesis.

4.3.2.1 Bayes’ theorem

It is possible to get from \(P(D \mid H_0)\) to \(P(H_0 \mid D)\), and hence to \(P(H_1 \mid D)\), but only with additional information, using Bayes’ theorem:

\[ P(H_0 \mid D) = \frac{P(D \mid H_0)\,P(H_0)}{P(D \mid H_0)\,P(H_0) + P(D \mid H_1)\,P(H_1)} \]

\[ P(H_1 \mid D) = 1 - P(H_0 \mid D) \]

The p-value supplies only one of the terms, \(P(D \mid H_0)\). Two further things are needed that a significance test does not use:

  • The prior probability of each hypothesis, \(P(H_0)\) and \(P(H_1) = 1 - P(H_0)\): how plausible the null was before the data were collected.
  • The probability of the data if the alternative is true, \(P(D \mid H_1)\). This is harder than it looks, because \(H_1\) (“the effect is not zero”) covers every possible non-zero effect size, and \(P(D \mid H_1)\) is different for each. It can only be calculated by assuming a particular effect size or a distribution of plausible effect sizes.

If \(D\) is defined as “a significant result” rather than “data at least as extreme as those observed”, these terms become familiar: \(P(D \mid H_0) = \alpha\), and \(P(D \mid H_1)\) is the study’s statistical power at the assumed effect size. \(P(H_0 \mid D)\) is then the probability that a significant result is a false positive, which depends on power and on the prior probability of \(H_0\) as much as on \(\alpha\).

Frequentist methods treat hypotheses as fixed states of the world rather than as things with probabilities, so they deliberately leave the prior out, and with it any answer to \(P(H_1 \mid D)\).

This lesson and course is not on frequentist vs. Bayesian approaches to Null Hypothesis Significance Testing - I mention it only to give you some awareness of where pursuing these questions lead to and where you could learn more if you are interested (e.g., Kruschke, 2015; McElreath, 2020). The point is that p-values cannot, by themselves, give the answer to researcher’s most common or intuitive questions. So, researchers have three options:

  • Change our statistical methods to answer our chosen question.
  • Stick with frequentist p-values and reconsider the questions we are (implicitly) asking.
  • Keep misinterpreting p-values and producing mushy partial truths [not this one, please].

4.3.3 p-values as incompatibility with the null model

It follows that a p-value is best read as a measure of the (in)compatibility between the data and the whole null model. It runs from 0 (the data are completely incompatible with the null model) to 1 (the data are exactly what the null model predicts), so the smaller the p-value, the greater the incompatibility (Greenland et al., 2016).

Lakens (2022) puts the same idea in plainer terms: a p-value tells us how surprising the observed data would be if the null model were true. Surprise depends on what the null model predicts, not only on the size of the observed effect. With a larger sample, the null model predicts mean differences that cluster more tightly around zero, so the same observed difference is more surprising, and its p-value smaller, with 5,000 participants than with 50. Laken’s ebook and online course ‘Improving your Statistical Inferences’ is highly recommended!

  • A small p-value says that the data would be unusual if every assumption of the null model were true. It does not say which assumption is false. \(H_0\) may be false, or the design may have been compromised, the distributional assumptions may not hold, or the result may have been selected for reporting because it was small.
  • A large p-value says only that the data are not unusual under the null model. It does not show that \(H_0\) is true: the same data would also be unremarkable under many other hypotheses, and a large p-value can itself result from violated assumptions.

This is why selective reporting matters so much. The ASA statement notes that when many analyses are conducted and only some are reported, the reported p-values become “essentially uninterpretable” (Wasserstein & Lazar, 2016). In null-model terms, the assumption that the result was not chosen for its p-value has been violated, so the reference distribution no longer applies.

4.3.3.1 What the ASA statements say

The 2016 ASA statement sets out six principles, summarised here:

  1. p-values can indicate how incompatible the data are with a specified statistical model.
  2. p-values do not measure the probability that the hypothesis studied is true, or the probability that the data were produced by random chance alone.
  3. Scientific conclusions and policy decisions should not be based only on whether a p-value passes a threshold.
  4. Proper inference requires full reporting and transparency.
  5. A p-value, or statistical significance, does not measure the size of an effect or the importance of a result.
  6. By itself, a p-value does not provide a good measure of evidence about a model or hypothesis.

The second principle rules out the most common textbook error identified by Cassidy et al. (2019). The claim that p is “the probability that the results occurred by chance” reverses the definition: the p-value is calculated assuming that chance alone is operating, so it cannot be the probability that chance alone is operating (Greenland et al., 2016).

A later statement from a task force convened by the ASA’s president (Benjamini et al., 2021) is more positive in tone. It stresses that p-values and significance tests, properly applied and interpreted, are valid tools that increase the rigour of conclusions and should not be abandoned. But it agrees on the essentials. p-values should be understood as assessments of observations relative to sampling variation, not as measures of practical significance, and any significance threshold should be chosen deliberately, based on the study’s goals and the consequences of wrong decisions.

4.4 How p-values are distributed

A good test of whether we understand p-values is whether we can say how they behave across many studies. Most researchers cannot answer two simple questions: if we ran the same study many times, what would the distribution of p-values look like (a) when the null hypothesis is true, and (b) when the alternative hypothesis is true?

  • When \(H_0\) is true (and the other assumptions of the null model hold), p-values are uniformly distributed between 0 and 1. Every value is equally likely. This follows directly from the definition: 5% of p-values fall below .05, 1% below .01, and 10% below .10. There is nothing special about .05; it is simply where we have chosen to draw the line.
  • When \(H_1\) is true, p-values are skewed towards zero, and increasingly so as the true effect size or the sample size increases. The proportion that fall below .05 is, by definition, the study’s statistical power.

One consequence is that p-values just below .05 are not particularly strong evidence against the null. In a study with 80% power, only about 3% of p-values fall between .04 and .05. In a study with 99% power, the figure is about 0.3%, which is less than the 1% expected when \(H_0\) is true (Lakens, 2022). A literature full of p-values between .01 and .05, from studies that claim to be well powered, is therefore itself a signal worth attending to.

function pCdf(x) {
  if (x <= 0) return 0
  if (x >= 1) return 1
  const z = normInv(1 - x / 2)
  return normCdf(delta - z) + normCdf(-delta - z)
}
function normCdf(x) {
  // Abramowitz & Stegun 7.1.26
  const t = 1 / (1 + 0.3275911 * Math.abs(x) / Math.SQRT2)

  return x >= 0 ? 0.5 * (1 + erf) : 0.5 * (1 - erf)
}

4.4.1 Distributions that cannot occur naturally

Because unbiased p-values are either flat (under \(H_0\)) or skewed towards zero (under \(H_1\)), there is no natural way for a collection of p-values to cluster just below .05. An excess of p-values just under the significance threshold requires some form of bias, such as publication bias or p-hacking. Masicampo and Lalande (2012) reported such a “peculiar prevalence” of p-values just below .05 in three psychology journals. The evidence is more mixed than that headline suggests: in a much larger sample of 258,050 test results, Hartgerink et al. (2016) found a bump below .05 in some journals, but it disappeared when p-values were recalculated from the reported test statistics. They did, however, find clear evidence of p-values being incorrectly rounded down to below .05.

The shape of a set of p-values is the basis of methods such as p-curve and z-curve. Their validity for diagnosing p-hacking in a given literature is debated, so they are not covered in this book, which focuses on methods for detecting impossible or implausible results within individual articles.

## (Mis)interpretation of *p*-values

Before assessing whether an article's conclusions follow from its results, it is worth checking whether we ourselves can reliably say what its results mean. The *p*-value is the most widely reported inferential statistic in psychology and health research, and it is also among the most widely misunderstood. This is not a problem confined to students: for four decades, surveys have found that most researchers, and most people who teach statistics, endorse at least one false statement about what a significant *p*-value tells them.

### Test yourself

The questionnaire below was first used by Oakes (1986) with academic psychologists, and has since been reused, with minor changes, in Israel (Falk & Greenbaum, 1995), Germany (Haller & Krauss, 2002), Spain (Badenes-Ribera et al., 2015), and China (Lyu et al., 2018, 2020). The wording here follows Haller and Krauss (2002).

Answer all six statements, then click the button at the bottom to see how you did.

::: {.webex-check .webex-box}
Suppose you have a treatment that you suspect may alter performance on a certain task. You compare the means of your control and experimental groups (say 20 subjects in each sample). Further, suppose you use a simple independent means *t*-test and your result is (*t* = 2.7, *d.f.* = 18, *p* = 0.01). Please mark each of the statements below as "true" or "false". "False" means that the statement does not follow logically from the above premises. Also note that one, several or none of the statements may be correct.

1.  You have absolutely disproved the null hypothesis (that is, there is no difference between the population means). `r webexercises::torf(FALSE)`
2.  You have found the probability of the null hypothesis being true. `r webexercises::torf(FALSE)`
3.  You have absolutely proved your experimental hypothesis (that there is a difference between the population means). `r webexercises::torf(FALSE)`
4.  You can deduce the probability of the experimental hypothesis being true. `r webexercises::torf(FALSE)`
5.  You know, if you decide to reject the null hypothesis, the probability that you are making the wrong decision. `r webexercises::torf(FALSE)`
6.  You have a reliable experimental finding in the sense that if, hypothetically, the experiment were repeated a great number of times, you would obtain a significant result on 99% of occasions. `r webexercises::torf(FALSE)`
:::

`r webexercises::hide("Why is each statement false?")`

All six statements are false. Each one corresponds to a recognisable fallacy.

1.  **Certainty (disproof).** A significance test gives a probabilistic statement about data, not a proof. Data this extreme occur under the null model 1% of the time, so the null hypothesis has not been disproved.
2.  **Inverse probability.** The *p*-value is the probability of data at least this extreme *given* the null model, $P(D \mid H_0)$. It is not the probability of the null hypothesis given the data, $P(H_0 \mid D)$. Getting from one to the other requires a prior probability for $H_0$, which a significance test does not use.
3.  **Certainty (proof).** The mirror image of statement 1. Rejecting the null does not prove the alternative, and even if a difference exists, it does not establish that the experimental hypothesis's explanation for it is correct.
4.  **Inverse probability, again.** The probability of the experimental hypothesis is no more deducible from *p* than the probability of the null is.
5.  **Error-rate confusion.** This reads *p* as the probability that rejecting $H_0$ is a mistake, i.e., $P(H_0 \mid \text{significant result})$. That is the false discovery risk, which depends on the prior probability of $H_0$ and on statistical power, neither of which is given. The *p*-value is also not the Type I error rate: α is fixed before the study and describes the long-run behaviour of the procedure, not the probability of error in this particular decision.
6.  **Replication fallacy.** 1 − *p* is not the probability of a significant replication. The replication probability depends on the true effect size and the replication's power. If the observed effect were exactly the true effect, a same-sized replication would be significant only about 70–75% of the time; and because significant results tend to overestimate effects, the real figure is usually lower.

#### How much should we trust these prevalence estimates?

The direction of this literature is robust, but the precise percentages should be treated with some caution. The questionnaires consist entirely of false statements, which means they cannot distinguish a respondent who misunderstands *p*-values from one who is uncertain, reads the items charitably, or assumes that at least one statement must be true. McShane and Gal (2017) also note that, as worded, some items can be read as questions about unknown population parameters rather than about the observed result. Similar criticisms have been made of the equivalent questionnaire for confidence intervals (García-Pérez & Alcalá-Quintana, 2016; Miller & Ulrich, 2016). Figures such as "90–99% of researchers endorse at least one false statement" are therefore better read as evidence that misinterpretation is common across fields, career stages, and countries than as well-measured prevalence estimates.

## What a *p*-value is

It's important to start by emphasizing that I and this book should not be considered the authoritative source of the definition and interpretation of *p*-values. For these, see the original sources cited below. 

### The formal definition

The definition found in most statistics textbooks descends from Fisher's significance tests and is stated formally by Cox (2006). Choose a test statistic *T* that measures how far the data depart from what the null hypothesis predicts. The *p*-value is the probability, calculated assuming the null hypothesis is true, of obtaining a value of *T* at least as extreme as the one actually observed:

$$
p = P(T \geq t_{\text{obs}};\ H_0)
$$

The semicolon rather than a conditional bar (\|) is deliberate. In the frequentist framework $H_0$ is not a random event with a probability of its own; it is an assumption under which the probability is calculated. Three ingredients are always involved:

1.  **A test statistic**, such as *t*, *F*, or χ², which summarises the data. For a two-sided test, *T* is the absolute value, e.g., \|*t*\|, so that departures in either direction count as extreme.
2.  **A reference distribution** for that statistic, derived by assuming $H_0$ is true.
3.  **A tail**: the probability of the observed value *or anything more extreme*, not of the observed value alone.

Applied to the quiz scenario: if there were truly no difference between the population means, and the other assumptions of the *t*-test held, a *t* statistic at least as far from zero as 2.7 would arise in about 1.5% of repeated studies with 18 degrees of freedom. (The questionnaire's *p* = .01 is rounded; the exact two-sided value for *t*(18) = 2.7 is .015.)

This definition is the key to every statement in the quiz. The *p*-value is calculated by *assuming* $H_0$ is true, so it cannot also be the probability that $H_0$ is true. As Greenland et al. (2016) put it, it is hard to see how a probability deduced from a set of assumptions could be the probability of those same assumptions.

#### "The model" is the null model

The American Statistical Association's statement on *p*-values (Wasserstein & Lazar, 2016) gives an informal definition that is often quoted:

::: big-quote
["the [long-run] probability under a specified statistical model that a statistical summary of the data ... would be equal to or more extreme than its observed value."]{.quote}

[American Statistical Association's statement on *p*-values (Wasserstein & Lazar, 2016).]{.citation}
:::

Many readers initially struggle with what "a specified statistical model" refers to. It is not the researcher's hypothesis, the alternative hypothesis, but rather the **null model**: the null hypothesis *together with* every other assumption needed to derive the reference distribution. The statement's first principle makes this explicit, describing the most common context as "a model, constructed under a set of assumptions, together with a so-called 'null hypothesis'". For the independent-samples *t*-test in the quiz, the null model includes:

- **The null hypothesis itself**: the population means are equal.
- **Distributional assumptions**: the outcome is normally distributed within each group, with equal variances.
- **Design assumptions**: observations are independent, and participants were randomly sampled or randomly assigned.
- **Analysis and reporting assumptions**: the test was planned before seeing the data, and this *p*-value was not selected for reporting from among many because it was small.

Greenland et al. (2016) build this into their definition.

::: big-quote
[The *p*-value is "the [long-run] probability that the chosen test statistic would have been at least as large as its observed value if every model assumption were correct, including the test hypothesis."]{.quote}

[Greenland et al. (2016)]{.citation}
:::

In their terms, $H_0$ is just the one assumption that has been *targeted* for testing (the "test hypothesis"); the *p*-value itself tests all of them at once.

### What we want to know versus what a *p*-value tells us: the inverse probability fallacy

Most of the misinterpretations in the quiz come from a mismatch between the question researchers want answered and the question a *p*-value actually answers.

**What most researchers want to know:**

::: big-quote
[Given my data,\
how probable is it that my hypothesis is true?]{.quote}
:::

This can be formalised as $P(H_1 \mid D)$, the probability of the alternative hypothesis given the data. Because the null and alternative hypotheses are mutually exclusive and exhaustive (the effect is either zero or it is not), this is the same question as how probable the null hypothesis is, since $P(H_1 \mid D) = 1 - P(H_0 \mid D)$.

**What a *p*-value actually tells us:**

::: big-quote
[Given that the null model is true,\
how improbable are data like mine?]{.quote}
:::

This can be formalised as $P(D \mid H_0)$, the probability of the data given the null hypothesis. Here $D$ is shorthand for "data at least as extreme as those observed", and the conditional bar is used loosely for "assuming", as it usually is in teaching (see the note on the semicolon above).

Significance testing then adds an inferential step that is not part of the probability calculation at all:

::: big-quote
[Given that the null model is true, how improbable are data like mine?\
If very improbable, we will act as if the alternative hypothesis is true.]{.quote}
:::

This decision rule is what $\alpha$ controls: in the long run, no more than 5% of tests of true null hypotheses will be significant (given the test's assumptions and no bias such as uncorrected multiple testing or *p*-hacking). It does not say how probable any particular hypothesis is.

The *p*-value, $P(D \mid H_0)$, and the quantity researchers want, $P(H_1 \mid D)$, differ in two ways, and misinterpretations usually slip across both at once:

1. **The direction of the conditional is reversed.** $P(D \mid H_0)$ is the probability of the data given the null; $P(H_0 \mid D)$ is the probability of the null given the data. These can differ enormously. The probability that a person is a smoker given that they have lung cancer is high; the probability that a person has lung cancer given that they smoke is much lower. Treating one as the other is known as the *inverse probability fallacy* or the *transposed conditional*.
2. **The complement is then taken.** Having read *p* as $P(H_0 \mid D)$, it is a short step to reading $1 - p$ as $P(H_1 \mid D)$: "*p* = .01, so there is a 99% chance the effect is real."

Quiz statement 2 is the first step: reading *p* as $P(H_0 \mid D)$. Statement 4 is both steps: reading $1 - p$ as $P(H_1 \mid D)$. Statement 5 reads *p* as $P(H_0 \mid \text{significant result})$, the probability that a rejection of $H_0$ is mistaken. All three treat a probability of data as a probability of a hypothesis.

#### Bayes' theorem

It is possible to get from $P(D \mid H_0)$ to $P(H_0 \mid D)$, and hence to $P(H_1 \mid D)$, but only with additional information, using Bayes' theorem:

$$
P(H_0 \mid D) = \frac{P(D \mid H_0)\,P(H_0)}{P(D \mid H_0)\,P(H_0) + P(D \mid H_1)\,P(H_1)}
$$

$$
P(H_1 \mid D) = 1 - P(H_0 \mid D)
$$

The *p*-value supplies only one of the terms, $P(D \mid H_0)$. Two further things are needed that a significance test does not use:

- **The prior probability of each hypothesis**, $P(H_0)$ and $P(H_1) = 1 - P(H_0)$: how plausible the null was before the data were collected.
- **The probability of the data if the alternative is true**, $P(D \mid H_1)$. This is harder than it looks, because $H_1$ ("the effect is not zero") covers every possible non-zero effect size, and $P(D \mid H_1)$ is different for each. It can only be calculated by assuming a particular effect size or a distribution of plausible effect sizes.

If $D$ is defined as "a significant result" rather than "data at least as extreme as those observed", these terms become familiar: $P(D \mid H_0) = \alpha$, and $P(D \mid H_1)$ is the study's statistical power at the assumed effect size. $P(H_0 \mid D)$ is then the probability that a significant result is a false positive, which depends on power and on the prior probability of $H_0$ as much as on $\alpha$.

Frequentist methods treat hypotheses as fixed states of the world rather than as things with probabilities, so they deliberately leave the prior out, and with it any answer to $P(H_1 \mid D)$.

This lesson and course is not on frequentist vs. Bayesian approaches to Null Hypothesis Significance Testing - I mention it only to give you some awareness of where pursuing these questions lead to and where you could learn more if you are interested (e.g., Kruschke, 2015; McElreath, 2020). The point is that *p*-values cannot, by themselves, give the answer to researcher's most common or intuitive questions. So, researchers have three options:

- Change our statistical methods to answer our chosen question.
- Stick with frequentist *p*-values and reconsider the questions we are (implicitly) asking.
- Keep misinterpreting *p*-values and producing mushy partial truths [not this one, please].

### *p*-values as incompatibility with the null model

It follows that a *p*-value is best read as a measure of the **(in)compatibility** between the data and the whole null model. It runs from 0 (the data are completely incompatible with the null model) to 1 (the data are exactly what the null model predicts), so the smaller the *p*-value, the greater the incompatibility (Greenland et al., 2016).

Lakens (2022) puts the same idea in plainer terms: a *p*-value tells us how *surprising* the observed data would be if the null model were true. Surprise depends on what the null model predicts, not only on the size of the observed effect. With a larger sample, the null model predicts mean differences that cluster more tightly around zero, so the same observed difference is more surprising, and its *p*-value smaller, with 5,000 participants than with 50. Laken's [ebook and online course 'Improving your Statistical Inferences'](https://lakens.github.io/statistical_inferences/) is highly recommended!

- **A small *p*-value** says that the data would be unusual if every assumption of the null model were true. It does not say which assumption is false. $H_0$ may be false, or the design may have been compromised, the distributional assumptions may not hold, or the result may have been selected for reporting because it was small.
- **A large *p*-value** says only that the data are not unusual under the null model. It does not show that $H_0$ is true: the same data would also be unremarkable under many other hypotheses, and a large *p*-value can itself result from violated assumptions.

This is why selective reporting matters so much. The ASA statement notes that when many analyses are conducted and only some are reported, the reported *p*-values become "essentially uninterpretable" (Wasserstein & Lazar, 2016). In null-model terms, the assumption that the result was not chosen for its *p*-value has been violated, so the reference distribution no longer applies.

#### What the ASA statements say

The 2016 ASA statement sets out six principles, summarised here:

1.  *p*-values can indicate how incompatible the data are with a specified statistical model.
2.  *p*-values do not measure the probability that the hypothesis studied is true, or the probability that the data were produced by random chance alone.
3.  Scientific conclusions and policy decisions should not be based only on whether a *p*-value passes a threshold.
4.  Proper inference requires full reporting and transparency.
5.  A *p*-value, or statistical significance, does not measure the size of an effect or the importance of a result.
6.  By itself, a *p*-value does not provide a good measure of evidence about a model or hypothesis.

The second principle rules out the most common textbook error identified by Cassidy et al. (2019). The claim that *p* is "the probability that the results occurred by chance" reverses the definition: the *p*-value is calculated *assuming* that chance alone is operating, so it cannot be the probability that chance alone is operating (Greenland et al., 2016).

A later statement from a task force convened by the ASA's president (Benjamini et al., 2021) is more positive in tone. It stresses that *p*-values and significance tests, properly applied and interpreted, are valid tools that increase the rigour of conclusions and should not be abandoned. But it agrees on the essentials. *p*-values should be understood as assessments of observations relative to sampling variation, not as measures of practical significance, and any significance threshold should be chosen deliberately, based on the study's goals and the consequences of wrong decisions.

## How *p*-values are distributed

A good test of whether we understand *p*-values is whether we can say how they behave across many studies. Most researchers cannot answer two simple questions: if we ran the same study many times, what would the distribution of *p*-values look like (a) when the null hypothesis is true, and (b) when the alternative hypothesis is true?

- **When $H_0$ is true** (and the other assumptions of the null model hold), *p*-values are **uniformly distributed** between 0 and 1. Every value is equally likely. This follows directly from the definition: 5% of *p*-values fall below .05, 1% below .01, and 10% below .10. There is nothing special about .05; it is simply where we have chosen to draw the line.
- **When $H_1$ is true**, *p*-values are **skewed towards zero**, and increasingly so as the true effect size or the sample size increases. The proportion that fall below .05 is, by definition, the study's statistical power.

One consequence is that *p*-values just below .05 are not particularly strong evidence against the null. In a study with 80% power, only about 3% of *p*-values fall between .04 and .05. In a study with 99% power, the figure is about 0.3%, which is *less* than the 1% expected when $H_0$ is true (Lakens, 2022). A literature full of *p*-values between .01 and .05, from studies that claim to be well powered, is therefore itself a signal worth attending to.

function pCdf(x) {
  if (x <= 0) return 0
  if (x >= 1) return 1
  const z = normInv(1 - x / 2)
  return normCdf(delta - z) + normCdf(-delta - z)
}
function normCdf(x) {
  // Abramowitz & Stegun 7.1.26
  const t = 1 / (1 + 0.3275911 * Math.abs(x) / Math.SQRT2)
  const erf = 1 - (((((1.061405429 * t - 1.453152027) * t) + 1.421413741) * t - 0.284496736) * t + 0.254829592) * t * Math.exp(-x * x / 2)
  return x >= 0 ? 0.5 * (1 + erf) : 0.5 * (1 - erf)
}
function normInv(p) {
  // Acklam's algorithm
  const a = [-3.969683028665376e+01, 2.209460984245205e+02, -2.759285104469687e+02, 1.383577518672690e+02, -3.066479806614716e+01, 2.506628277459239e+00]
  const b = [-5.447609879822406e+01, 1.615858368580409e+02, -1.556989798598866e+02, 6.680131188771972e+01, -1.328068155288572e+01]
  const c = [-7.784894002430293e-03, -3.223964580411365e-01, -2.400758277161838e+00, -2.549732539343734e+00, 4.374664141464968e+00, 2.938163982698783e+00]
  const e = [7.784695709041462e-03, 3.224671290700398e-01, 2.445134137142996e+00, 3.754408661907416e+00]
  const plow = 0.02425
  if (p < plow) {
    const q = Math.sqrt(-2 * Math.log(p))
    return (((((c[0] * q + c[1]) * q + c[2]) * q + c[3]) * q + c[4]) * q + c[5]) / ((((e[0] * q + e[1]) * q + e[2]) * q + e[3]) * q + 1)
  }
  if (p > 1 - plow) return -normInv(1 - p)
  const q = p - 0.5, r = q * q
  return (((((a[0] * r + a[1]) * r + a[2]) * r + a[3]) * r + a[4]) * r + a[5]) * q / (((((b[0] * r + b[1]) * r + b[2]) * r + b[3]) * r + b[4]) * r + 1)
}

### Distributions that cannot occur naturally

Because unbiased *p*-values are either flat (under $H_0$) or skewed towards zero (under $H_1$), there is no natural way for a collection of *p*-values to cluster just below .05. An excess of *p*-values just under the significance threshold requires some form of bias, such as publication bias or *p*-hacking. Masicampo and Lalande (2012) reported such a "peculiar prevalence" of *p*-values just below .05 in three psychology journals. The evidence is more mixed than that headline suggests: in a much larger sample of 258,050 test results, Hartgerink et al. (2016) found a bump below .05 in some journals, but it disappeared when *p*-values were recalculated from the reported test statistics. They did, however, find clear evidence of *p*-values being incorrectly rounded down to below .05.

The shape of a set of *p*-values is the basis of methods such as *p*-curve and *z*-curve. Their validity for diagnosing *p*-hacking in a given literature is debated, so they are not covered in this book, which focuses on methods for detecting impossible or implausible results within individual articles.
